♻️ The rock cycle — how rocks turn into each other

Igneous rocks cool from melted rock. Cool it slowly deep underground and the crystals grow big (granite, gabbro); cool it fast at the surface and they stay tiny (rhyolite, basalt). 🌋

Sedimentary rocks are built from bits: sand, mud, pebbles or shells settle in layers, get buried and are cemented together. They're the only rocks that hold fossils. 🐚

Metamorphic rocks are older rocks cooked and squeezed — never melted, just changed. Limestone → marble, shale → slate → schist → gneiss, sandstone → quartzite. 🔥

Melt a metamorphic rock and it becomes magma again. Round and round, for billions of years. ♻️

🧰 The detective's five tests (and why colour is a liar)

1. Hardness. Find the softest thing that scratches it: fingernail (2½), copper coin (3½), iron nail (4½), glass (5½), steel file (6½). That's the Mohs scale, and it's the single most useful test there is.

2. Streak. Rub the mineral on unglazed tile and look at the powder colour. Hematite looks silvery-black but its streak is always rust red. Streak only works on minerals — a rock is a mixture, so its powder is a muddle.

3. Acid. A drop of vinegar makes calcite fizz. That's limestone, chalk, marble and calcite itself — and nothing else in this collection.

4. Magnet. Only magnetite jumps onto a magnet. (Basalt gives a faint tug, because it has specks of magnetite in it.)

5. How it breaks. Cleavage means it splits along flat faces — mica peels into sheets, halite breaks into cubes. Fracture means it breaks any old way — quartz and obsidian break in curved, razor-sharp shells.

And colour is the worst clue of all: quartz comes in a dozen colours, and pyrite is exactly the colour of gold. Test, don't guess! 🕵️
